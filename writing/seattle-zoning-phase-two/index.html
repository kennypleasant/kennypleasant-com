Zoning

Seattle passed a growth plan.
It has not passed the zoning.

14 September 2026 By Kenny Pleasant 7 min read

On 30 September, a light rail station opens in north Seattle. Two blocks away, the upzoning that was supposed to arrive with it has quietly disappeared from the map.

That is not a conspiracy. It is how this process actually works, and almost nobody outside the land use world understands the difference between what Seattle adopted and what Seattle has actually changed. The gap between those two things is where people are currently making expensive assumptions about what their property is worth.

What actually passed, and what did not

On 17 December 2025, the Seattle City Council voted 8 to 0 to adopt the new Comprehensive Plan, which guides growth through 2044. It arrived nearly a year past the state deadline.

A Comprehensive Plan is a policy framework. It states where the city intends growth to go. It does not, by itself, change what you are allowed to build on your lot. The zoning map changes that do that are separate, and Seattle is doing them in four packages.

Phase one is done. Adopted the same night, it rewrote the standards for Neighborhood Residential zones, which cover close to three quarters of Seattle's residential land, and legalized middle housing citywide: townhomes, duplexes, cottage housing and stacked flats. That one is real, it is in effect, and it is the change I wrote about in your lot may be worth more than your house.

Phase two is not done. The centers and corridors package was transmitted to Council on 28 January 2026 and announced by the Mayor's office the following day as the final proposal. It covers 30 new neighborhood centers, expanded urban centers, and upzones along frequent transit corridors. As of this writing it is still awaiting a Council vote.

Phases three and four have no schedule at all. They cover the dense urban centers, places like Northgate, Capitol Hill and the Alaska Junction, and they are waiting on environmental review.

If someone tells you what your property will be worth "under the new zoning," ask them which phase they are talking about. If the answer is anything other than Neighborhood Residential, they are describing a proposal.

The map has been shrinking

This is the part that matters most and gets the least coverage. The phase two proposal is not the same as the phase two draft, and it is considerably smaller.

The neighborhood center count went from roughly 50 originally proposed down to the 30 that moved forward. Nine additional centers and six center expansions were pushed into a later phase, where they now require a supplemental environmental impact statement before they can be adopted at all. That is not a delay of weeks.

Specific transit corridor upzones were cut between drafts. Reporting by The Urbanist on the zoning maps released 30 January 2026 documented upzones removed entirely along the Route 2 corridor heading toward Lake Washington in Madrona, upzones disappearing across much of the Montlake stretch of E 24th Street on the Route 48 corridor, Route 49 in north Capitol Hill scaled back sharply, and 10th Avenue E removed.

Where corridor upzones survived, they are modest. Low-Rise 3, which permits about five stories, extending roughly half a block off the corridor. That is a meaningful change for the parcels it touches and close to irrelevant for the ones it does not.

Which brings us back to that station

Pinehurst Station opens Wednesday, 30 September, just north of NE 130th Street. It is served by both the 1 Line and the 2 Line, which combined means a train every four to five minutes between Lynnwood and the International District. Sound Transit projects 3,400 daily boardings. King County Metro launched Route 77 this fall specifically to feed it from Lake City and Bitter Lake.

By any normal reading, that is a neighborhood about to be re-rated. Transit access is one of the few amenities that reliably moves land value, because it changes who can live there and how they get to work.

And in the phase two maps, two entire blocks of zoning changes along Densmore Avenue North in Haller Lake, near that station, were dropped without a stated explanation.

There is also a physical constraint that no amount of zoning fixes. Jackson Park is a 160 acre publicly owned golf course sitting immediately northeast of the station, and it breaks the walkshed. A station's catchment is not a circle drawn on a map. It is the set of homes someone can actually walk from, and a golf course is a wall.

What this means if you own near a station area

Your land has two values right now and they are moving at different speeds.

The first is what it is worth today under adopted zoning. If you are in a Neighborhood Residential zone, that number already changed in January, and it changed in your favor. That is banked.

The second is what it might be worth if phase two passes as proposed, or if a later phase reaches you. That number is a bet on a document that has been cut once already and can be cut again. Builders know this. It is why an offer on a development parcel today will often be structured with a feasibility period long enough to see what Council does, rather than priced as though the upzone is certain.

If you are being offered a premium today on the assumption of an upzone that has not been adopted, that premium is the buyer sharing their risk with you. Sometimes that is a good trade. It is never a free one.

What this means if you are buying

Buying near a station that opens before its rezone lands is a real strategy, and it is not a crazy one. The transit is certain. The density is not. You are buying the amenity at a price that has not yet absorbed the upside, and accepting that the upside may never arrive.

What you should not do is pay the price the upzone would justify before the upzone exists. That is paying for both outcomes and receiving one.

Three questions worth answering before you write an offer in any station area:

  • What is the adopted zoning on this parcel today, not the proposed zoning. These are different documents and only one of them is enforceable.
  • Is this parcel inside a phase two boundary, and did that boundary survive from the earlier draft to the January proposal. Several did not.
  • Can someone actually walk from this parcel to the platform, or is there a golf course, a freeway or a ravine in the way. Seattle has all three.

The most common mistake I am seeing this year is treating "Seattle upzoned" as a single event that already happened. It is four events, one has happened, one is a proposal, and two are unscheduled.

What I would actually do

If you own in a Neighborhood Residential zone, get a current valuation that accounts for the January change. Most of the comparable sales your automated estimate is using predate it, which is why those estimates are frequently wrong in a specific and predictable direction right now.

If you own near a future or new station, do nothing quickly. The station opening is a known date. Council action is not. Selling into the excitement before the zoning resolves means selling the option along with the land.

If you are buying, price the adopted rules and treat any upzone as free optionality rather than as value you pay for. And read the phase two boundary map yourself rather than trusting a summary, including this one.

One honest caveat about the timing. Council can act on phase two at any point, and adoption was originally targeted for the summer. Before you make a decision on any of this, confirm the current status rather than relying on an article, including this one. Land use timelines in this city have a long history of moving.
