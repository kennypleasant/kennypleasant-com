Off-Market Land

The upzone is a year away.
Price the land like it.

18 November 2026 By Kenny Pleasant 7 min read

If you own land in Seattle, or you are trying to buy some, you have probably heard that the city is about to upzone 30 new neighborhood centers and the bus corridors around them. Six story buildings where today you can only do townhomes. A lot of people are already pricing land as if that is done.

It is not done. It is not close to done. Based on everything on the public record right now, the earliest that zoning can take effect is next fall, and there are real ways it slips past that. If you pay today for zoning that does not exist yet, you are the one carrying the cost of the wait.

Here is where it actually stands, what you can build today, and how I would price a parcel in the meantime.

What happened

Seattle adopted its new Comprehensive Plan and passed the first round of zoning, the middle housing rules, which took effect on 21 January 2026. The second round, called Centers and Corridors, is the one that creates the 30 new neighborhood centers and the corridor upzones. That is the one everybody is pricing in.

On 9 September the state Growth Management Hearings Board ruled on challenges to the plan. The board mostly sided with the city. But it found the environmental impact statement was too narrow. The city studied the development it thought was likely, around 120,000 new homes over 20 years. The board said it had to study the maximum that was possible under the plan. According to The Urbanist's reporting on the decision, that leaves about 42,000 potential homes the city never analyzed.

The board gave the city one year to fix it. That deadline is 9 September 2027, with a compliance hearing later that fall. The city's own planning office says it will release a draft of the new analysis in 2027, finish it for the board by September 2027, and only then restart council review of Centers and Corridors. In the meantime that legislation is paused. The city is also appealing the board's ruling in court.

On top of that, two citizen appeals of the same environmental review are still alive at the city's hearing examiner. If the current schedule holds, arguments start on 8 February 2027. Councilmember Eddie Lin has said he wants to bring legislation to expedite those appeals and put them to rest, but as of The Urbanist's 6 November report it had not been introduced.

Why "next fall" is the optimistic case

The Urbanist's read is that neighborhood center and corridor zoning could take effect next fall at the earliest. I agree with the word earliest. For that to happen, the city has to finish a large new environmental analysis on time, get it through the board, get the council to restart and pass the legislation, and survive whatever appeals come next. Every step in this process so far has taken longer than the city planned. The first estimate was that Centers and Corridors would pass this past summer.

I am not saying it never happens. Seattle is going to grow and the state is pushing it to. I am saying nobody can tell you the month, and you should not pay a seller for a month nobody can promise.

Zoning that has not passed is not a feature of the land. It is a bet on a timeline. Pay for what you can build today. Treat the upzone as upside you get for free if it comes.

What you can build today

The good news is that the rules already in effect are better than most people realize. Under the middle housing rules that took effect in January, most former single family lots are now Neighborhood Residential. On those lots you can build the greatest of a few numbers: one unit per 1,250 square feet of lot, or four units, or six units if the lot is within a quarter mile of a major transit stop or at least two units are affordable.

Stacked flats, meaning apartment or condo style units on top of each other, get a much denser allowance of one unit per 600 square feet of lot, with a floor area ratio of 1.8, or 2.0 if you keep certain trees or hit a green factor score. Within a half mile walk of a major transit stop, no parking is required at all.

Run that on a standard 5,000 square foot Seattle lot. This is my math on the city's published rules, not a feasibility study. At one unit per 600 square feet you get eight stacked flats. At a floor area ratio of 1.8 you get about 9,000 square feet of building, so roughly 1,125 square feet per unit. That is a real project on land that was zoned for one house a year ago.

Whether it pencils depends on construction cost, financing and what those units sell or rent for. But the point is this: there is real value you can underwrite today, under rules that are already law. That is what I would pay for.

What waiting actually costs

Say a seller wants a $200,000 premium because their lot is inside a proposed neighborhood center. If the zoning that justifies that premium is two years out, you are carrying that $200,000 the entire time without being able to build anything bigger than what is allowed today.

My calculation, with simple assumptions: $200,000 at 7.11%, which is the current Freddie Mac 30 year average, for two years is about $28,440 in carrying cost. That is not counting taxes, insurance, or the fact that land loans almost always cost more than a home mortgage. And that assumes the zoning comes, comes at the density you expected, and the map still includes your lot. Earlier this year the city trimmed corridor upzones off the map in several neighborhoods before anything was even voted on.

If you are selling land

This cuts both ways, and I would rather tell you now than after your listing sits.

If your price only works because of zoning that has not passed, the buyers who know this market will not pay it. The builders and developers I work with underwrite what they can permit. Some will pay something for the upside, but not full price for it, and not with no conditions.

What you can do is show a buyer exactly what is buildable today, with the numbers, and let the future zoning be the reason they move faster instead of the reason they pay more. If you want to hold out for the upzone, that is a fair choice. Just make it on purpose, knowing the earliest date is next fall and could be later. I covered how lot value and house value can move separately in this earlier piece on lot value, and the history of these zoning phases in the growth plan breakdown.

What I would actually do

  • Price every parcel on what the current Neighborhood Residential rules allow today. Unit count, floor area, parking, transit distance. Get those numbers before you talk price.
  • If a seller is asking for an upzone premium, ask what date they are pricing to. Then calculate your carrying cost to that date, and to a year after it.
  • If you do pay for upside, structure it. A lower base price with a payment that only kicks in if the zoning passes protects both sides better than a big number up front.
  • Check whether the parcel is within a quarter mile of a major transit stop. That one fact moves you from four units to six under today's rules, and removes parking within a half mile.
  • Watch three dates: 8 February 2027 for the hearing examiner arguments, the 2027 draft analysis release, and 9 September 2027 for the board deadline. Any one of them slipping pushes everything after it.
  • If you are selling, lead with today's buildable numbers. A buyer who can underwrite your lot this week is worth more than one who has to wait for city council.

If you own land in King, Snohomish or Pierce County, or you are looking for an infill parcel to build on, I am glad to run what is actually buildable on a specific lot with you. That is the conversation that should come before a price.
