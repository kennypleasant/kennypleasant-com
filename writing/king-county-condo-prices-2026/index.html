Condo prices fell 15.5%.
Check the building first.

19 November 2026 By Kenny Pleasant 7 min read

The median condo in King County sold for $465,000 in October. A year ago it was about $550,000. That is a 15.5% drop in twelve months, and it is the biggest move in any part of this market right now.

A lot of people are going to look at that number and decide condos are the deal of the year. Some of them will be right. But when condo prices fall this much faster than house prices, the first question is not how cheap the unit is. It is what is going on with the building.

Here is what the numbers say, what changed in condo lending this year, and what I would check before I made an offer on anything with an HOA.

What the October numbers say

The NWMLS October report, published 5 November, put the King County condo median at $465,000, down 15.5% from a year ago. King County condos are sitting at 7.0 months of inventory. Across the whole NWMLS area the condo median was $435,000, down 13.0%, the lowest since February 2023.

Compare that with houses. The King County single family median was $925,000, down 7.2%. Across the NWMLS area, single family homes had 4.14 months of inventory against 6.59 for condos. So condos are falling about twice as fast as houses, and there is a lot more of them sitting unsold.

Seven months of inventory is a buyer's market by any normal definition. If you are buying a condo, you have leverage. If you are selling one, you have a lot of competition.

What that means for the payment

These are my calculations, not a lender quote. I took the King County condo median now and backed out what the median was a year ago from the 15.5% drop, which comes to about $550,300. Assumptions: 20% down, 30 year fixed, principal and interest only. I used Freddie Mac's 7.11% average from 12 November for today and 6.24%, the same survey a year earlier, for last year.

Today: a $372,000 loan at 7.11% is about $2,502 a month. A year ago: about a $440,200 loan at 6.24% was about $2,708 a month. So even with higher rates, the condo median costs about $205 a month less to own than it did last year. That is not true for houses right now. The price drop on condos was big enough to beat the rate increase.

That is the good news. It leaves out HOA dues, and HOA dues are where this gets serious.

What changed in condo lending this year

Most buyers do not know this, and most listings will not tell you. When you get a conventional loan on a condo, the lender is not only approving you. It is approving the building. Fannie Mae tightened those building rules this year, in a lender letter published 18 March 2026.

Here is what changed.

The light review is gone. Fannie Mae retired its Limited Review process for loan applications dated on or after 3 August 2026. Limited Review was the lighter version of the building check. Most buildings now go through the Full Review, unless they qualify for a waiver, which Fannie expanded to projects with ten or fewer units.

Reserves have to be funded at the high end. If the lender relies on the building's reserve study, the HOA budget now has to include the highest recommended reserve contribution in that study. The baseline funding method, where the reserve balance can run down close to zero, is no longer allowed.

The reserve minimum goes up on 4 January. For Full Review projects, the minimum share of the annual budget that goes to reserves rises from 10% to 15%, for loan applications dated on or after 4 January 2027. That is six weeks away.

Insurance deductibles are capped. The maximum per unit deductible on the building's master policy is $50,000 for applications on or after 1 July 2026. If there is a per unit deductible, your own unit policy can have a deductible no higher than the greater of 5% of coverage or $2,500.

Why that matters to price

This next part is my read, not something the data proves. I cannot tell you how much of the condo drop comes from these rules versus rates, inventory and buyers simply preferring houses. But I can tell you how the mechanics work.

A building that does not meet these rules is hard to finance with a conventional loan. A building that is hard to finance has fewer buyers. Fewer buyers means lower prices and longer days on market. And a building that has been underfunding reserves has two ways to fix it: raise dues, or hit owners with a special assessment. Either one lands on whoever owns the unit at the time.

So a condo that looks cheap might be cheap because the building is in good shape and the market is soft. Or it might be cheap because the building is about to cost a lot more. The price alone does not tell you which one you are looking at.

When you buy a condo you are buying a share of a building's finances. A low price on a building with thin reserves is not a discount. It is a bill that has not arrived yet.

What Washington law gives you

Washington buyers have real protection here, if they use it.

Under RCW 64.90.545, associations covered by the law have to update their reserve study every year, and every third year it has to be done by a reserve study professional with a site visit. There are exemptions, including when the cost of the study would be more than 10% of the association's annual budget, so do not assume every building has one.

Under RCW 64.90.640, the seller has to provide a resale certificate. It has to say whether the association has a reserve study, list any special assessments levied, disclose unsatisfied judgments and the status of legal actions against the association, and describe the building's insurance. Once you first receive it, you generally have five business days to cancel. That is your window. Use all of it.

If you own a condo

If you are thinking about selling, talk to your board now about where the reserves stand against the 15% minimum. A buyer's lender is going to look. If your building is close, that is a strength you should be marketing. If it is short, you want to know before a buyer's lender tells you in the middle of escrow. Lower listing prices do not fix a financing problem. Only the building can fix that.

And price to the market you are actually in. With seven months of supply in King County, your unit is competing with a lot of others, and buyers can afford to be picky about the building.

What I would actually do

  • Before you write an offer, ask your lender whether the building has already been reviewed, and whether it meets Fannie Mae's current project standards. Do not wait for underwriting to find out.
  • Get the reserve study, the current budget and the last two years of board minutes. Minutes are where special assessments get talked about before they get voted on.
  • Check what share of the budget goes to reserves. If it is under 15%, ask the board what the plan is before 4 January 2027.
  • Read the master insurance policy deductible. If it is a per unit deductible, make sure your own policy covers it.
  • When the resale certificate arrives, read it the same day. You have five business days to cancel. Any special assessment, pending litigation or missing reserve study is a reason to slow down.
  • Use the inventory. At seven months of supply you can negotiate price, ask the seller to cover an assessment, or ask for credits. Do it.
  • If you are comparing a condo to a house, include the HOA dues in the payment, and assume they will go up.

If you are looking at a condo in King, Snohomish or Pierce County and want a second set of eyes on the building before you commit, I am glad to go through it with you. That is worth doing before the price conversation, not after.
