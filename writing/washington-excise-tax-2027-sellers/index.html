Sellers

The January tax break is $494.
Do not list around it.

24 November 2026 By Kenny Pleasant 7 min read

On 1 January 2027, Washington moves the brackets on its real estate excise tax. If you are selling a home in King, Snohomish or Pierce County, you are going to hear about it, and some people will tell you to hold your closing until January to save money.

I ran the numbers. On a $1.5 million sale in Seattle, closing in January instead of December saves you $46.80. The most anyone can save on a single sale is $494, and you only get that if you are selling for more than about $3 million.

That is not a reason to wait. But the excise tax itself is a real number, it is bigger than most sellers expect, and it changes how you should think about price. That part is worth ten minutes.

What actually changes on 1 January

The real estate excise tax, which everyone calls REET, is a tax on the sale of real property. The Department of Revenue says the seller usually pays it. If the seller does not, the buyer is on the hook, and an unpaid tax can become a lien on the property.

The state portion is graduated. Each slice of the price is taxed at its own rate, the same way income tax brackets work. By law the dollar thresholds are adjusted every four years. The rates do not change. Only the lines between them move.

Here is what the Department of Revenue published for sales starting 1 January 2027, with the current thresholds in brackets:

  • 1.1% on the first $551,000 (now $525,000)
  • 1.28% from there up to $1,551,000 (now $1,525,000)
  • 2.75% from there up to $3,051,000 (now $3,025,000)
  • 3.0% on everything above $3,051,000 (now $3,025,000)

Every line moves up by $26,000. That is the whole change.

What the change is worth to you

These are my calculations from the Department of Revenue's published brackets. They cover the state portion only.

If you sell for $1,525,000 or less, the only thing that changes for you is the first bracket. An extra $26,000 gets taxed at 1.1% instead of 1.28%. That saves you $46.80. It does not matter whether you sell for $851,250, which was the King County median in October, or for $1.5 million. The answer is $46.80.

If you sell between about $1.55 million and $3 million, two brackets move in your favor and you save $429. Above $3,051,000, all three move and you save $494. That is the ceiling.

Now compare that to what one month of waiting costs. I do not know your loan, so here is a simple example: if you still owe $500,000 at 3%, one month of interest is about $1,250. That is before property tax, insurance, utilities and the lawn. The holding cost of one extra month beats the tax savings at every price point.

What the tax actually costs you

This is the part most sellers have never seen written out. On top of the state tax there is a local REET. In the DOR's current local rate table, almost every city in King, Snohomish and Pierce County charges 0.50%, including Seattle, Bellevue, Everett and Tacoma, plus the unincorporated parts of all three counties. Skykomish is the exception at 0.25%.

So on a $1.5 million sale in Seattle in 2027, my math comes to $18,208 in state tax plus $7,500 local. That is $25,708, about 1.71% of the price. On a $2 million sale it is $41,208, about 2.06%. On the $925,000 King County median house it is a little over $15,000.

That money comes out of your proceeds at closing. If your net sheet does not have it on a line of its own, get a new net sheet.

The excise tax change is a rounding error. The excise tax is not. On a $2 million Seattle sale it is more than $41,000, and it comes out of your check.

The bracket that matters at the top of this market

A lot of what I sell lands right around $1.5 million. That is exactly where the brackets start to bite.

Below $1,551,000 in 2027, each extra dollar of price is taxed at 1.28% state plus 0.50% local in most cities. Above it, each extra dollar is taxed at 2.75% plus 0.50%. That is 3.25%. Every $10,000 of price between $1,551,000 and $3,051,000 costs you $325 in excise tax.

Two things follow from that.

A price cut up there costs you less than it looks. If you drop from $1,650,000 to $1,600,000, you give up $50,000 in price but you also pay $1,625 less in excise tax. Your net drops by about $48,375, not $50,000. That does not make cutting fun. It does make the conversation more honest.

Do not get cute with the bracket. I would never move value out of the price on paper to stay under a line. The tax is on the selling price, the Department of Revenue audits these transactions, and it requires you to keep documentation supporting the price for at least four years. Saving a few thousand dollars is not worth a tax problem with penalties and interest attached.

Why timing still matters, just not for this

Here is the market you would be selling into. The NWMLS October report, published 5 November, showed active listings across its service area up 25.2% from a year ago. In King County inventory was up 32.8%. New listings were flat with last October, at 7,991. Pending sales fell 17.8%.

Read those together. The extra inventory is not a wave of new sellers. It is homes that are not selling. The NWMLS said the same thing. Months of inventory across King, Snohomish and Pierce rose to 4.19, from 2.66 a year earlier.

Rates are not helping. Freddie Mac's survey put the 30 year fixed at 7.28% on 19 November, up from 7.11% the week before and 6.26% a year ago. That is national data, but it is what your buyer is looking at.

What I tell sellers: list when your house is ready and your price is right, not when a calendar or a tax bracket says to. A well priced home in December gets the buyers who are actually serious, because nobody tours houses the week before Christmas for fun. A home priced off last spring's comps will sit in December, sit in January, and still need a price cut in March. Waiting does not fix price.

If you are already under contract

For the excise tax, the Department of Revenue says the date of sale is the date the deed is signed and delivered to the buyer, which in practice is the date it is notarized. Not the recording date. The tax is due on that date.

So if you are set to close in the last few days of December and you are selling above $1.55 million, pushing the deed into January would save you $429 or $494. Ask, if it costs nothing. But I would not reopen a signed deal or risk a buyer's rate lock over it. Your buyer is borrowing at over 7%. Their lock expiring is a far bigger problem than your $429.

What I would actually do

  • Do not hold your listing or your closing for 1 January. The most the change can save you is $494, and one month of carrying the house costs more.
  • Get a net sheet that shows the excise tax as its own line, state and local, at your actual price. On a $1.5 million Seattle sale expect about $25,700.
  • If you are pricing above $1,551,000, know that every $10,000 of price above that line carries $325 in excise tax in most local cities. Use that when you weigh a price cut against a credit.
  • Never restructure the price on paper to stay under a bracket. Keep every document supporting the sale price for four years.
  • If you are closing in late December above $1.55 million, ask about a January deed date only if it costs the buyer nothing. Do not trade certainty for $429.
  • Price for the market in the October numbers, not for last spring. Inventory is up by a third in King County and the extra homes are the ones that did not sell.
  • If the property is classified farm or timberland, or you are transferring an interest in an entity that owns real estate, the rules are different. Talk to a tax professional before you assume any of the math above.

If you are thinking about selling in King, Snohomish or Pierce County this winter or next spring and want to see what you would actually walk away with, I am glad to sit down and run it with you. The number on the net sheet is the one that matters.
